The dataset contains COVID-19-related tweets labeled with one of five sentiment classes. 1. Sentiment Class Distribution

Positive is the largest training class (11,422; 27.75%), while Extremely Negative is the smallest (5,481; 13.32%). The dataset is moderately imbalanced, but no single class dominates. Accuracy should therefore be reported alongside macro-F1, which weights all five classes equally.

2. Tweet Length and Text Characteristics

Tweets contain informal social-media text, including URLs, hashtags, mentions, and punctuation. Mean tweet length is 204.20 characters and 30.50 whitespace-separated words in the training set, compared with 213.44 characters and 32.91 words in the test set. The observed maximum is 64 words in training and 62 words in testing.

Preprocessing will preserve sentiment-bearing signals such as negation, punctuation, and hashtags. Token sequences will be truncated and padded consistently, with padding excluded from meaningful model computation through attention masks or sequence-length handling.

3. Temporal Distribution Shift

The supplied test set primarily predates the training set: test dates span 2–16 March 2020, whereas training dates span 16 March–14 April 2020. Tweet volume also varies substantially by date. Because COVID-19 discussion and public sentiment can change over time, test performance may reflect temporal distribution differences as well as model quality.

4. Duplicate and Missing-Value Checks

Tweet texts and sentiment labels have no missing values. The preliminary normalized-text check found 15 repeated texts within the training set, no repeated normalized texts within the test set, and one normalized text shared across the two splits (approximately 0.03% of unique test texts). No conflicting sentiment labels were found for normalized duplicates. This overlap should be documented when reporting final evaluation results.

Baseline, Pretrained Backbone & Fine-Tuning Protocol

The study compares three models on five-class tweet sentiment classification: a BiLSTM trained from scratch (EXP-1), DistilBERT with a frozen backbone where only the classification head is trained (EXP-2), and DistilBERT with full fine-tuning (EXP-3). All three share the same tokenizer, data splits, random seed, and checkpoint-selection rule, so differences in results come from the model and the training scope rather than from the data pipeline.

Unified Experiment Activity Diagram

Tokenize → split → train → validate → restore the best checkpoint → test once.

Data Preparation
  • Input / target: OriginalTweet → Sentiment (5 classes)
  • Tokenizer: distilbert/distilbert-base-uncased (lowercased WordPiece), shared by all three models
  • Sequence length: truncated to 128 tokens; padded dynamically to the longest tweet in each batch
  • Padding handling: attention mask for DistilBERT; true sequence lengths derived from the mask for the BiLSTM
  • Validation: stratified 10% subset held out from the training file (37,041 train / 4,116 validation, seed 42), used only for early stopping and checkpoint selection
  • Test: supplied test file, evaluated once with the selected checkpoint
  • Text handling: no cleaning; raw tweets are tokenized as-is, so negation, punctuation, hashtags, mentions, and links are preserved
  • Augmentation: none
Shared Training Configuration
  • Optimizer: AdamW (weight decay 0.01)
  • Loss: CrossEntropyLoss (no class weights)
  • Gradient clipping: max norm 1.0
  • Early-stopping patience: 3 epochs
  • Checkpoint criterion: highest validation macro-F1
  • Random seed: 42 (Python, NumPy, PyTorch)
  • Mixed precision: not used
  • Device: first available of CUDA, MPS, CPU
  • Hardware: NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU (8 GB)

Fine-Tuning Protocol

Frozen backbone (EXP-2)

  • Gradients are disabled for the embeddings and all six encoder layers.
  • The backbone runs in evaluation mode during training, so its dropout does not perturb the frozen features; the head stays in training mode.
  • The optimizer and gradient clipping only see the 594,437 trainable head parameters.

Full fine-tuning (EXP-3)

  • All 66,957,317 parameters are updated with AdamW.
  • The learning rate warms up linearly over the first 10% of steps, then decays linearly to zero.
  • Warm-up and the small learning rate (2e-5) limit early updates that could damage pretrained representations.
  • The classification head dropout is 0.3; the encoder keeps DistilBERT’s default dropout.
Comparison scope

EXP-2 and EXP-3 use the same learning rate, schedule, batch size, epoch budget, seed, and selection rule, so the set of trainable parameters is the only factor that changes. EXP-1 differs from both in architecture, pretraining, and hyperparameters, because a randomly initialized recurrent model needs a larger learning rate and more epochs.

Interpreting test results

The supplied test split predates the training split and shares one normalized text with it (EDA Sections 3 and 4). Test scores may therefore reflect temporal distribution shift as well as model quality, and the validation subset, not the test set, is used for every training decision.

Comparison from the completed runs
  • Full fine-tuning (EXP-3) is the best model: 85.33% test accuracy and 0.8577 macro-F1, which is 12.16 accuracy points and 0.1156 macro-F1 above the BiLSTM baseline. Its test loss is 0.4941, against 0.8570 for the baseline.
  • The frozen backbone (EXP-2) reaches only 38.94% accuracy and 0.3779 macro-F1. That is 34.23 accuracy points below the from-scratch BiLSTM and 46.39 points below full fine-tuning.
  • EXP-2’s best validation macro-F1 (0.3893) came at its final epoch (5 of 5), so training had not plateaued when the epoch budget ended. Its score reflects the shared 2e-5 learning rate and five-epoch budget as much as the limits of frozen features; a larger learning rate or more epochs for the head would likely raise it, which this controlled setup does not test.
  • Validation macro-F1 is higher than test macro-F1 for every model: by 2.4 points for EXP-1, 2.2 for EXP-3, and 1.1 for EXP-2. This is consistent with selecting checkpoints on validation data and with the temporal shift described in EDA Section 3, but these runs cannot separate the two effects.
  • Early stopping: the BiLSTM’s best epoch was 7 and training stopped after epoch 10. Full fine-tuning peaked at epoch 4 of 5. The frozen run used all 5 epochs.

Per-class observations
  • Pretraining helps every class. Moving from the BiLSTM to full fine-tuning raises F1 by 0.055 on Neutral (the smallest gain) up to 0.138 on Negative (the largest).
  • The weakest class differs by model: Positive for the BiLSTM (F1 0.6990) and for full fine-tuning (0.8320), and Extremely Negative for the frozen run (0.2775, with recall of only 0.1875).
  • The same precision/recall pattern appears in all three models. Both extreme classes have precision above recall, so they are predicted conservatively, while Positive and Negative have recall above precision, so they are over-predicted.
  • The confusion matrices show that errors mostly fall between neighboring classes: adjacent-class confusions make up 72.0% of the BiLSTM’s errors, 80.4% of full fine-tuning’s, and 59.4% of the frozen run’s. Learning Curves

Training Remarks

EXP-1 BiLSTM

  • Training loss falls from 1.4382 to 0.1345 and training accuracy rises from 35.9% to 95.7% over 10 epochs, while validation macro-F1 climbs from 0.4267 to its peak of 0.7658 at epoch 7. In epochs 1–2 training accuracy is below validation accuracy (35.9% vs 43.1%, then 52.6% vs 55.1%) because it is averaged while the model is still improving.
  • Validation loss is lowest at epoch 5 (0.7443) and rises afterwards, with a small dip at epoch 7, reaching 0.9702 by epoch 10 while training loss keeps falling. The macro-F1-selected checkpoint (epoch 7, validation loss 0.7774) therefore sits slightly after the loss minimum.
  • The gap between training and validation accuracy is 14.7 points at the selected epoch (90.6% vs 75.9%) and 20.0 points at epoch 10 (95.7% vs 75.7%). Validation accuracy stays between 75.0% and 75.9% from epoch 7 on. Most of the model’s parameters (3.9 of 4.57 M) are in the randomly initialized embedding table, which can memorize the 37,041 training tweets.
  • Validation macro-F1 stayed below 0.7658 for epochs 8–10 (0.7632, 0.7573, 0.7628), so training stopped after epoch 10 and the epoch-7 checkpoint was restored.

EXP-2 DistilBERT (frozen backbone)

  • The best validation macro-F1 (0.3893) came at epoch 5 of 5, so the run had not passed its peak when the five-epoch budget ended and early stopping never applied.
  • After all five epochs the frozen run’s validation macro-F1 (0.3893) is still far below the 0.7654 that full fine-tuning reached after a single epoch.
  • Only the 594,437 head parameters update, and they start from random initialization at the small learning rate (2e-5) chosen for fine-tuning a pretrained backbone, so the head probably moves too slowly to converge in five epochs. This is why EXP-2 should be read as a frozen backbone under the same budget as EXP-3, not as the best a frozen backbone can do.
  • Test macro-F1 (0.3779) is 1.1 points below the best validation score, the smallest gap of the three models.

EXP-3 DistilBERT (full fine-tuning)

  • After a single epoch validation macro-F1 is already 0.7654, nearly the BiLSTM’s best (0.7658), and validation accuracy reaches 84.9% at epoch 2. Pretrained representations transfer almost immediately once the backbone is allowed to adapt.
  • Validation macro-F1 peaks at 0.8794 (validation accuracy 87.7%) at epoch 4, but validation loss is lowest one epoch earlier (0.3863) and then rises (0.4082, 0.4821) while training loss keeps falling (0.3019, 0.2195, 0.1628), the onset of overfitting.
  • The training–validation accuracy gap is 5.4 points at the selected epoch (93.1% vs 87.7%) and 8.1 points at epoch 5, against 14.7 points for the BiLSTM at its selected epoch.
  • Epoch 5 did not improve (1/3), so the five-epoch budget ended before the patience limit was reached, and the epoch-4 checkpoint was restored.
Across the three runs
  • EXP-1 and EXP-3 overfit; EXP-2 had not peaked. Both models that adapt their embeddings or backbone show rising validation loss while training loss falls, whereas the frozen run’s best checkpoint is its final epoch.
  • Pretraining accelerates learning once the backbone adapts. After the first epoch, validation macro-F1 is 0.7654 for EXP-3, against 0.4267 for EXP-1; the frozen run’s best score after five epochs (0.3893) is still well below EXP-3’s epoch-1 value.
  • The checkpoint rule picks a later epoch than the loss minimum. For EXP-1 and EXP-3, the best macro-F1 epochs (7 and 4) come after the validation-loss minima (5 and 3), at the cost of slightly higher validation loss.
  • Epoch cost differs widely. An epoch takes about 23–26 s for the BiLSTM and 203–205 s for full fine-tuning, while the frozen run averages 66.3 s (331.45 s over 5 epochs).

Limitations & Conclusion

  • Limited repeatability: each experiment is a single run with seed 42 and no confidence intervals, and runtime figures depend on the hardware listed in the Methodology tab, so absolute times are indicative.
  • Untuned frozen run: EXP-2 reuses the full fine-tuning learning rate and epoch budget so that trainable scope is the only changed factor. Its best epoch is the last one, so it is probably under-trained and does not show the best achievable frozen-backbone result.
  • Not a single-factor comparison for EXP-1: the BiLSTM differs from the DistilBERT runs in architecture, pretraining, learning rate, batch size, and epochs, so the gap reflects their combined effect.
  • Evaluation caveats: the test split predates the training split and shares one normalized text with it, and some labels are ambiguous between adjacent sentiment classes (see the EDA tab), so part of the remaining error may not be model failure.
  • Approximate FLOPs: thop counts layer operations only, so the DistilBERT FLOPs figure is a lower bound.
  • Best measured performance: EXP-3 (full fine-tuning) achieves 85.33% test accuracy and 0.8577 macro-F1.
  • Different practical trade-offs: the BiLSTM is the smallest and fastest model (4.57 M parameters, 0.0585 ms/sample) at 73.17% accuracy; freezing the backbone cuts training time by 67% but not inference cost, and it was the weakest model here.
  • Conclusion for this setup: under a shared 2e-5 learning rate and five-epoch budget, updating the whole DistilBERT backbone was necessary to benefit from pretraining, and it beat the from-scratch BiLSTM by a clear margin.

What the failure breakdown shows
  • Mostly graded mistakes, not reversals. For the BiLSTM and full fine-tuning, intensity errors are the largest category (50.8% and 48.7% of errors), while polarity flips account for 27.3% and 18.7%. Almost all of those flips are mild: 253 of 278 for the BiLSTM and 97 of 104 for full fine-tuning.
  • Extreme classes are under-called. 65.8% of the BiLSTM’s intensity errors, 62.4% of full fine-tuning’s, and 82.9% of the frozen run’s predict the milder neighbor. For example, the BiLSTM labels 198 of 599 Extremely Positive tweets (33.1%) as Positive, against 90 (15.0%) for full fine-tuning.
  • Pretraining does not help every category equally. Compared with the BiLSTM, full fine-tuning removes 62.6% of polarity flips and 47.7% of intensity errors, but only 18.4% of neutral-boundary errors, so the Neutral boundary improves least.
  • The frozen run fails differently. Polarity flips are its largest category (880 errors, 37.9%), including 298 severe flips and 22 direct Extremely Negative ↔ Extremely Positive swaps. It predicts Positive for 371 of 1,041 Negative tweets (35.6%), which suggests the frozen features carry sentiment polarity only weakly.
  • Neutral is handled differently by each model. The BiLSTM pushes Neutral tweets into polar classes far more often than it pulls polar tweets into Neutral (163 vs 60), while full fine-tuning does the opposite (115 polar tweets predicted as Neutral vs 67 the other way).

What the cases show
  • Graded errors, no reversals. Four of the six failures are intensity errors and two are neutral-boundary errors; none is a polarity flip. This is in line with the breakdown above, where polarity flips are only 27.3% (BiLSTM) and 18.7% (full fine-tuning) of errors.
  • Disagreements go both ways. DistilBERT under-calls the intensity in the first case and over-calls it in the second, while the BiLSTM errs in the third and fourth. The even split is by construction (two cases per category), not evidence of equal accuracy: DistilBERT is 12.16 accuracy points better overall.
  • Hardest when there is no overt emotion. Both models fail on the tweets that report facts or ask a question (the fifth and sixth cases), where the assigned label (Neutral, Extremely Positive) depends on annotator judgment. This matches the labeling consideration in the EDA tab.
  • Noisy surface text. All eight tweets contain hashtags, and several carry HTML-entity residue such as & amp ; and & gt ;, because the pipeline applies no text cleaning.

Cost versus accuracy
  • Full fine-tuning buys accuracy at a moderate training cost. Compared with the BiLSTM it adds 12.16 accuracy points for 771.7 s (12.9 minutes) more training, about 0.95 points per extra GPU-minute, but needs 14.7× the weight memory (255.42 vs 17.42 MB) and 20.3× the inference time per sample.
  • Freezing saves time and memory but not enough to matter here. The frozen run trains 3.07× faster than full fine-tuning (558.8 vs 181.7 samples/s) and its estimated training state is 3.9× smaller (262 MB vs 1.0 GB). Those savings of 687.6 s (11.5 minutes) and about 760 MB cost 46.39 accuracy points, and inference is no cheaper.
  • Early stopping cost is visible. The three patience epochs spent after the BiLSTM’s best checkpoint took 29.5% of its training time, and the one epoch after full fine-tuning’s best checkpoint took 20.0% of its time.
  • The BiLSTM uses hardware less efficiently per FLOP. It reaches about 1.46 counted TFLOP/s, 4.8× lower than DistilBERT’s 7.0 (roughly 10–15% of the GPU’s FP32 peak, against 48–71% for DistilBERT), which is consistent with the recurrence being less parallel than Transformer matrix multiplications. It is still far cheaper in absolute terms: 17,088 samples/s at inference against about 845.
Limits of this compute analysis
  • Peak GPU memory, GPU utilization percentage, and power draw were not measured, so memory figures are analytic estimates, and utilization is only approximated from effective FLOP rates against a peak that depends on the laptop’s power limit.
  • Each experiment was timed once, and inference was timed at the evaluation batch sizes, not at batch size 1.
  • FLOPs come from the thop library, which counts layer operations only, so the DistilBERT FLOPs and its effective rate are lower bounds.
